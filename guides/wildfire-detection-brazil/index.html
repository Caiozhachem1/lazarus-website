Wildfire detection systems in Brazil

Satellites, camera towers, ground sensors and people on the ground each find a fire at a different size and after a different delay. This guide compares them and shows how they work together.

In short

No single system finds every fire early. Public satellites cover the whole country at no cost, but see a fire only once it has grown. Camera towers find the smoke column from kilometres away, soon after it rises into view. Ground sensors catch a fire where it starts, seconds after ignition and before there is smoke, over a short range. The strongest setups layer all three and send every alert to one map.

Public satellites

Brazil’s base layer is public. INPE’s Programa Queimadas reads about 200 satellite images a day from 11 satellites and publishes fire detections, called focos, for the whole country.1 NASA’s FIRMS publishes detections from the VIIRS sensors worldwide within about three hours of each pass.2

A detection means there is fire somewhere inside one image pixel. A VIIRS pixel is 375 metres on a side, so a fire has to grow before it shows, and cloud or thick smoke hides the ground.

Heat inside an image pixel hundreds of metres wide
How soon
At each pass, or every ten minutes from the GOES satellite at coarser detail
Reach
The whole country, with no equipment
Limit
Small fires, cloud and smoke

Camera towers

A tower with a camera and on-board AI watches the horizon for smoke and turns a zoom lens on it. It needs a line of sight: the smoke has to rise above the terrain and the canopy before the camera can find it. Night work needs a thermal camera or an illuminator.

A Lazarus Sentinel detects smoke at up to 10 km. It runs on solar power and connects over 4G or Starlink.

Sees
A smoke column in line of sight
How soon
Soon after the smoke rises into view
Reach
Kilometres around each tower
Limit
Terrain, distance and darkness

Ground sensors

Small sensors placed along a fence, a road or a forest edge pick up the gases of combustion and the sound of fire close to where it starts, before there is smoke for a camera to see. Each one covers a short distance, so they go where fires are likely to start.

Lazarus Scouts detect combustion and smoldering seconds after ignition and cue the nearest Sentinel to look.

Sees
Combustion gases and sound near the sensor
How soon
Seconds after ignition
Reach
A short distance per sensor
Limit
Only where sensors are installed

People on the ground

Lookouts, patrols, brigades and calls from neighbours still report fires. They depend on someone being in the right place, which is hardest at night and in remote country.

Sees
Smoke or flame within view
How soon
When someone is there to see it
Reach
As far as the patrol goes
Limit
Nights and remote areas

Why Lazarus detects first

Lazarus is built for best-in-class, extremely early detection of forest fires and wildfires, and to be right about it. Scout sensors detect combustion seconds after ignition, before there is visible smoke, wherever the gases reach them. Sentinel towers detect smoke up to 10 km away. Our own detector reads the public satellites’ raw scans every ten minutes; in our measurements it called 64% of fires before NASA, a median of 47 minutes ahead.

The accuracy comes from fusing the sensors. Every detection is analysed by AI on the device, analysed again on our servers and cross-checked against the other sensors before an alert goes out, so false alarms are rare.

All three layers report to one map, where brigades coordinate the response in Overwatch. More than 50 Sentinel stations stand in Brazil, with over 250,000 hectares under watch.

How to choose

Start from what a late detection would cost. For very large areas, satellite alerts are the low-cost base. Where a fire must be caught in minutes, such as plantations, protected areas, power lines and land near communities, add towers with a view over the ground. Where fires usually start, along roads, fence lines and boundaries, add ground sensors.

Common questions

What is the fastest way to detect a wildfire?

A sensor close to the ignition. Ground sensors that pick up combustion gases can raise an alert seconds after a fire starts, before there is a smoke column for a camera or enough heat for a satellite. Lazarus Scouts do exactly that.

How accurate are automatic wildfire alerts?

It depends on how many independent signals confirm each detection. A camera alone can mistake dust, fog or a reflection for smoke. Lazarus analyses each detection on the device and again on its servers, and cross-checks it against gas and acoustic sensors, which keeps false alarms rare.

Are INPE and NASA fire alerts enough?

They are the right base layer: free and nationwide. But a satellite detection means fire somewhere inside a pixel hundreds of metres wide, and NASA publishes VIIRS detections within about three hours of a pass, so a fire can be well established by the time it appears.

How far can a camera tower detect smoke?

It depends on the camera, the tower height and the terrain. A Lazarus Sentinel detects smoke at up to 10 km in line of sight.

Can these systems work without grid power or internet?

Yes. Lazarus Sentinels and Scouts run on solar power and connect over 4G, Starlink and LoRa radio, and satellite alerts need no equipment on site.
